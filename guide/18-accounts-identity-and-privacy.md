# 18. Accounts, Identity, and Digital Privacy

Protect the accounts that can unlock the rest of your life: email, banking, phone service, and recovery accounts. Prevention and a small recovery plan work together.

## 18.1 Secure important accounts and preserve a recovery path

Start with your main email and financial accounts: use unique passwords stored in a reputable password manager, enable multifactor authentication, and install security updates. Prefer a security key or authenticator app where supported. Store recovery codes safely, separately from the device you might lose. Back up important files and test recovering a sample. No single tool prevents every attack; never approve an unexpected login request merely to make repeated prompts stop.

- **Cost and effort:** Free options exist; estimate one to two hours initially. Hardware keys and additional backup storage cost extra.
- **Payoff:** Reduces common account-takeover risks and makes a lost phone or failed device easier to recover from.
- **Priority:** Start here.
- **Basis:** Guidance and Practice. [FTC account and device protection](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/protect-your-personal-information-hackers-and-scammers); [FTC phishing defenses and backups](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-recognize-avoid-phishing-scams).

## 18.2 Freeze credit when you are not applying for it

Request a free security freeze separately with Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion, using the official bureau links in FTC guidance. Store access credentials securely. Before a legitimate application, ask which bureau must be unfrozen and arrange a temporary lift. A freeze limits access used for many new-credit applications; it is not comprehensive identity-theft protection and does not secure existing bank accounts. Continue checking statements and credit reports, and distinguish a statutory freeze from optional paid monitoring products.

- **Cost and effort:** Placing and lifting freezes is free; estimate 30–60 minutes for three bureaus and recordkeeping.
- **Payoff:** Adds an obstacle to fraudulent new-credit applications without lowering your credit score.
- **Priority:** Start here.
- **Basis:** Rule and Guidance. [FTC credit freezes and fraud alerts](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/credit-freezes-and-fraud-alerts).

## 18.3 Start an identity-theft recovery file immediately

If someone uses your identity, contact the affected company's fraud department through a verified channel, report the theft at IdentityTheft.gov, and follow its recovery steps. Review credit reports and consider freezes or fraud alerts. Keep report numbers, disputed accounts, dates, names, and copies of everything submitted in one secure file. Tell each affected institution rather than assuming the FTC report contacts them all. Tax, medical, benefits, and financial identity theft may require different follow-up processes.

- **Cost and effort:** Official reporting is free; estimate one to two hours initially, with potentially substantial follow-up.
- **Payoff:** Creates an organized record for disputes and helps you address the specific misuse sooner.
- **Priority:** When relevant.
- **Basis:** Guidance and Practice. [USAGov identity-theft reporting](https://www.usa.gov/identity-theft); [FTC recovery planning](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/protect-your-personal-information-hackers-and-scammers).

## 18.4 Verify urgent messages through a channel you already trust

When a message demands a payment, login, or verification code, pause and open the organization's app or type its known address yourself. Contact it using a number from an existing statement. If you already entered credentials, change them through the real service and secure any reused accounts. If an attachment may have installed malware, update security software and run a scan; report exposed financial or identity information through the relevant institution and recovery process.

- **Cost and effort:** Verification usually takes minutes; recovery effort depends on what information or device access was exposed.
- **Payoff:** Reduces reliance on logos, caller ID, urgency, or polished writing as evidence of authenticity.
- **Priority:** Start here.
- **Basis:** Guidance and Practice. [FTC phishing response](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-recognize-avoid-phishing-scams); [FTC multifactor authentication](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/use-two-factor-authentication-protect-your-accounts).

## 18.5 Separate online trust from requests for money or personal data

Review what strangers can learn from your public profile, photos, location posts, and quizzes, then reduce unnecessary exposure. Be cautious when an online relationship quickly moves off-platform or turns into an urgent money request or investment pitch. Do not send money or gifts to a romantic contact you have never met in person. If you paid a suspected scammer, contact the payment provider immediately and ask about recovery; refunds are not guaranteed.

- **Cost and effort:** Free; estimate 30 minutes for a privacy review and a pause before sensitive requests.
- **Payoff:** Limits material for impersonation and makes pressure tactics easier to recognize before money leaves your account.
- **Priority:** Build next.
- **Basis:** Guidance and Practice. [FTC romance scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-romance-scams); [USAGov identity exposure](https://www.usa.gov/identity-theft).
