# 04. Health Insurance and Medical Bills

In the United States, the same care can produce very different bills depending on your plan, provider, and paperwork. Learn your actual coverage and keep records of consequential decisions.

## 04.1 Compare a full year of costs before choosing a plan

Compare annual premiums, deductibles, copays, coinsurance, and the out-of-pocket maximum using each plan's Summary of Benefits and Coverage. Estimate both an ordinary year and a year with substantial covered care. Check your doctors, hospitals, and prescriptions before choosing. Premiums, noncovered services, and some out-of-network charges generally sit outside the out-of-pocket limit. A low premium is useful only if the plan also fits your care needs and available cash.

- **Cost and effort:** Planning estimate: 60–90 minutes during enrollment; gather last year's usage and your current medication list.
- **Payoff:** Helps you choose coverage you can use and budget for, without assuming the cheapest premium means the cheapest care.
- **Priority:** When relevant.
- **Basis:** Rule; Practice. [HealthCare.gov: Compare total health costs](https://www.healthcare.gov/choose-a-plan/your-total-costs/).

## 04.2 Check enrollment options when life changes

After losing coverage, moving, marrying, or having a child, check promptly whether you qualify for a Special Enrollment Period. Eligibility, documentation, and deadlines depend on the event and coverage source. Medicaid and CHIP accept applications year-round, with state-specific eligibility rules. Start at HealthCare.gov or your state's official marketplace and coordinate effective dates before canceling existing coverage. Revisit the choices at renewal because plans, prices, and household circumstances change.

- **Cost and effort:** Planning estimate: 30–60 minutes for an initial application review; collecting documents and resolving eligibility may take longer.
- **Payoff:** Helps prevent avoidable gaps in coverage and missed opportunities for assistance.
- **Priority:** When relevant.
- **Basis:** Rule; Practice. [HealthCare.gov: Special Enrollment Periods](https://www.healthcare.gov/coverage-outside-open-enrollment/special-enrollment-period/). [HealthCare.gov: Medicaid and CHIP](https://www.healthcare.gov/medicaid-chip/getting-medicaid-chip/).

## 04.3 Confirm the network and expected charges before planned care

Before nonemergency care, ask both the insurer and provider about your exact plan, clinician, facility, and any separate laboratory or imaging service. Record names, dates, and reference numbers. Ask about prior authorization and a written cost estimate. If uninsured or self-paying, check your right to a good faith estimate for scheduled care. Federal surprise-billing protections have exceptions, including many ground-ambulance bills; review the applicable rules rather than assuming every charge is protected.

- **Cost and effort:** Planning estimate: 15–30 minutes for a straightforward service; complex procedures may require several calls.
- **Payoff:** Helps identify avoidable billing surprises and creates a record if a coverage dispute arises.
- **Priority:** When relevant.
- **Basis:** Rule; Practice. [HealthCare.gov: Confirm your providers](https://www.healthcare.gov/using-marketplace-coverage/getting-medical-care/). [CMS: Medical bill rights](https://www.cms.gov/initiatives/your-patient-rights/medical-bill-rights).

## 04.4 Request bill review and financial assistance before borrowing

Compare the provider's itemized bill with your insurer's explanation of benefits and question duplicate or unfamiliar charges. Ask the hospital for its financial assistance policy, application, deadlines, and which clinicians it covers. Tax-exempt hospitals must have such policies, but eligibility and included providers vary. Request a billing hold while an application is reviewed and confirm any arrangement in writing. Consider assistance and affordable payment options before moving medical debt onto a credit card.

- **Cost and effort:** Planning estimate: one hour to gather bills and start an application; follow-up time varies.
- **Payoff:** May correct errors or reduce eligible charges and helps you avoid borrowing before assistance is considered.
- **Priority:** When relevant.
- **Basis:** Rule; Practice. [IRS: Hospital financial assistance policies](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/financial-assistance-policies-faps). [CFPB: Reviewing and resolving medical bills](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/blog/seven-ways-to-keep-medical-debt-in-check/).

## 04.5 Appeal a denial using the reason and deadline

Read the denial notice and identify the exact reason: missing information, a coverage exclusion, medical necessity, or another issue. Ask the clinician's office for relevant records and submit an internal appeal through the plan's stated process. Keep a copy and proof of submission. Ask about expedited review if delay could seriously harm your health. If the denial stands, check eligibility for independent external review; the process differs across private plans, Medicare, and Medicaid.

- **Cost and effort:** Planning estimate: one to three hours for an initial organized appeal; medical records and additional reviews may take longer.
- **Payoff:** Gives a disputed decision a documented review without assuming that the first denial is final.
- **Priority:** When relevant.
- **Basis:** Rule; Practice. [HealthCare.gov: Appealing an insurer's decision](https://www.healthcare.gov/appeal-insurance-company-decision/).
