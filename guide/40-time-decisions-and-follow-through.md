# 40 Time decisions and follow-through

These are practical experiments, not clinical or financial guarantees. Keep the ones that improve your actual week.

## 40.1 Choose one urgent protection and one recurring improvement

First handle a real deadline or a threat to safety, housing, essential care, or income. Then choose one repeatable improvement such as reviewing a recurring bill or making a realistic meal plan. Write the next action and when you will do it. Leave the rest of this guide for later. A list of two achievable actions is easier to evaluate than a promise to overhaul your whole life.

- **Cost and effort:** Free; ten minutes to choose is an estimate.
- **Payoff:** Turns a large reference guide into a manageable next step.
- **Priority:** Start here.
- **Basis:** Practice. [Editorial decision method](../METHODOLOGY.md)

## 40.2 Compare a purchase in money, time, and risk separately

Write the upfront price, recurring charges, maintenance, exit costs, and time required. Add what could go wrong and whether you can absorb it. Use your own assumptions and test a less favorable case. For example, a cheaper apartment may add commuting time and a car expense. Do not collapse health, relationships, and dollars into a fake universal score; decide which tradeoffs matter to your household.

- **Cost and effort:** Free worksheet; effort rises with the size of the decision.
- **Payoff:** Makes hidden commitments visible before you sign.
- **Priority:** Build next.
- **Basis:** Practice. [Editorial decision method](../METHODOLOGY.md)

## 40.3 Run a one-week experiment on a recurring time drain

Choose one friction point such as repeated errands or distracted work. Record the current pattern briefly, try one change for a week, and compare time, stress, and results. Examples include batching errands or silencing nonessential alerts during a short work block. Keep emergency and caregiving communication available. If the change creates more work than it saves, revise or drop it rather than buying a new productivity system.

- **Cost and effort:** Free; brief tracking is an editorial suggestion.
- **Payoff:** Helps you discover what works in your circumstances.
- **Priority:** Build next.
- **Basis:** Practice. [Editorial decision method](../METHODOLOGY.md)

## 40.4 Set a review date and a stop rule for experiments

Before a course, subscription, side project, or new routine, define what success would look like and when you will reassess. Decide what would make you stop, change approach, or seek help. Separate a modest reversible trial from a major commitment. Money already spent is a record of the past; the next payment should be judged on the likely value from here.

- **Cost and effort:** Free planning; trial costs depend on the activity.
- **Payoff:** Limits repeated spending on a plan that is not meeting its purpose.
- **Priority:** Build next.
- **Basis:** Practice. [Editorial decision method](../METHODOLOGY.md)

## 40.5 Keep a small household administration file

Keep a secure index of policies, benefit notices, warranties, important contacts, and upcoming renewal or appeal dates. Store sensitive documents in protected storage, not in a public repository or shared checklist. Give an appropriate trusted person a way to locate essential information. Schedule a brief review after a move, job change, birth, separation, or death; revise the system if maintaining it becomes a burden.

- **Cost and effort:** Free or low-cost storage; recurring review time is modest but variable.
- **Payoff:** Reduces searching and missed tasks during an already difficult event.
- **Priority:** Build next.
- **Basis:** Practice. [Editorial decision method](../METHODOLOGY.md)
