# 03. Emergencies and First Aid

These entries help you prepare and recognize when to act. Hands-on first-aid training and a dispatcher's live instructions matter more than remembering a page of text.

## 03.1 Call 911 promptly and give a usable location

Call 911 when someone needs immediate emergency assistance. State the exact location, callback number, and what happened, then answer the dispatcher's questions and follow instructions. Put the phone on speaker if you need your hands free. At home, make sure household members know the address, unit number, and access details. Keep an emergency contact and medication list accessible without delaying a call to gather documents.

- **Cost and effort:** Planning estimate: 10 minutes to prepare household information. Ambulance and emergency treatment charges depend on circumstances and coverage.
- **Payoff:** Helps responders find you and lets the dispatcher guide immediate care while help is being sent.
- **Priority:** Start here.
- **Basis:** Guidance; Practice. [911.gov: Calling 911](https://www.911.gov/calling-911/).

## 03.2 Learn CPR and find the nearest AED

Take a CPR/AED course and notice where AEDs are located at work and places you visit. If a teen or adult suddenly collapses, is unresponsive, and is not breathing normally, call 911, send someone for an AED, and begin chest compressions as directed. For this situation, hands-only CPR is a useful bystander response. Turn on the AED and follow its prompts; training should also cover when rescue breaths matter.

- **Cost and effort:** Planning estimate: a few hours for training; fees vary, and some community sessions are free.
- **Payoff:** Prepares you to provide immediate help during cardiac arrest before emergency responders arrive.
- **Priority:** Build next.
- **Basis:** Guidance. [American Heart Association: What is CPR?](https://cpr.heart.org/en/resources/what-is-cpr). [American Heart Association: Recognizing when CPR is needed](https://www.heart.org/en/news/2026/06/01/when-is-the-right-time-to-perform-cpr).

## 03.3 Treat stroke and heart-attack symptoms as time-critical

Call 911 for sudden facial droop, arm weakness, speech difficulty, or other sudden stroke signs, even if they improve. Note when symptoms began or when the person was last well. Chest pressure, shortness of breath, or pain spreading to the arm, jaw, or back can signal a heart attack; symptoms are not always dramatic. Do not drive yourself or wait for certainty. Let emergency professionals direct the next steps.

- **Cost and effort:** Preparation estimate: 10 minutes to review warning signs with family; emergency care costs vary.
- **Payoff:** Reduces delay in reaching treatments for conditions where prompt medical evaluation matters.
- **Priority:** Start here.
- **Basis:** Guidance. [CDC: Stroke signs and symptoms](https://www.cdc.gov/stroke/signs-symptoms/index.html). [NHLBI: Heart attack symptoms](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/heart-attack/symptoms).

## 03.4 Save Poison Help and call before symptoms appear

Save Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222. If a medicine, household chemical, or other substance may have poisoned someone, call promptly rather than waiting for symptoms. Have the product container, approximate exposure time, and the person's age available if possible. For someone who is not breathing or is in immediate danger, call 911 first. Avoid home treatments such as activated charcoal unless a poison professional specifically directs you.

- **Cost and effort:** The Poison Help call is toll-free. Planning estimate: two minutes to save the number and share it with caregivers.
- **Payoff:** Provides exposure-specific advice and helps determine what level of care is needed.
- **Priority:** Start here.
- **Basis:** Guidance. [HRSA Poison Help: What you can do](https://poisonhelp.hrsa.gov/what-you-can-do).

## 03.5 Practice how to control severe bleeding

Take an ACS Stop the Bleed course so you can recognize dangerous bleeding and practice direct pressure, wound packing, and a tourniquet. In an emergency, check that the scene is safe, call 911, and apply firm, continuous pressure to the bleeding wound with gauze or a clean cloth. Use protective gloves if available. Follow dispatcher instructions and your training; specialized techniques need correct placement and continued pressure.

- **Cost and effort:** Planning estimate: about an hour for introductory training, plus practice; class and supply costs vary by location.
- **Payoff:** Gives you practical actions to limit life-threatening blood loss while emergency help is coming.
- **Priority:** Build next.
- **Basis:** Guidance. [American College of Surgeons: Stop the Bleed course FAQ](https://www.stopthebleed.org/about/faq/). [American College of Surgeons: Bleeding-control instructor guide](https://www.stopthebleed.org/media/pkpnanvp/acs-stop-the-bleed-version-30-instructor-guide.pdf).
