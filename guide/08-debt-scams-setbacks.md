# 08. Debt, Scams, and Financial Setbacks

A setback calls for a sequence of decisions, not self-blame. Protect essential living needs, verify what you owe, and ask for help before signing a new financial commitment.

## 08.1 Protect essentials when there is not enough money

Write down what you can pay this month and the consequences of each missed bill. Give serious attention to housing, utilities, food, necessary transportation, insurance, and court-ordered obligations. Contact providers before deadlines, explain the shortage, and request an affordable arrangement in writing. Do not simply pay whoever calls most often. If eviction, repossession, or a lawsuit is threatened, seek local legal or housing help promptly; a budget cannot resolve those deadlines by itself.

- **Cost and effort:** Free to make the plan; estimate one hour plus calls. Assistance eligibility, legal fees, and payment arrangements depend on your location and circumstances.
- **Payoff:** Directs limited cash toward the consequences that could most disrupt your safety, housing, and ability to earn.
- **Priority:** When relevant.
- **Basis:** Guidance; Practice. [CFPB: Prioritizing bills worksheet](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_your-money-your-goals_prioritizing-bills_tool.pdf).

## 08.2 Give extra debt payments a specific target

Once essential expenses and required payments are covered, list each current debt's balance, interest rate, minimum, and promotional expiration. Direct affordable extra payments toward the highest-cost debt if minimizing interest is your goal. Paying a smaller balance first may help you stick with the plan, but can cost more. Keep making other required payments and preserve a workable cash buffer. Revisit the order when rates change or a hardship makes the plan unrealistic.

- **Cost and effort:** Free planning; estimate 30–45 minutes, then a monthly review. Extra payments use available cash, so the amount must fit your real budget.
- **Payoff:** Gives each extra dollar a purpose and makes payoff progress easier to track without assuming new borrowing will solve the problem.
- **Priority:** Build next.
- **Basis:** Guidance; Practice. [CFPB: Debt action plan](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_your-money-your-goals_debt-action-plan_tool_2018-11.pdf).

## 08.3 Verify collection demands before paying

Ask for the collector's validation information and compare the creditor, amount, and account with your records. For collectors covered by federal debt-collection rules, a written dispute within the notice's 30-day period generally requires a pause in collection of the disputed amount until an adequate response. Keep dated copies and delivery evidence. Do not ignore court papers. Get legal advice before paying an old debt: state limitation periods and the effects of payment differ.

- **Cost and effort:** Usually free apart from possible postage; estimate 30–60 minutes to assemble records. Responding to a lawsuit may require legal help and has separate deadlines.
- **Payoff:** Helps prevent payment of the wrong debt and preserves a record if you need to challenge collection activity.
- **Priority:** When relevant.
- **Basis:** Rule; Guidance. [CFPB: Debt validation notices](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-information-does-a-debt-collector-have-to-give-me-about-the-debt-en-331/), [CFPB: Lowering debt and old-debt cautions](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/documents/10044/cfpb_lowering-your-debt_tool_2021-08.pdf).

## 08.4 Compare qualified help before buying debt relief

Start with your creditor's hardship department or a reputable nonprofit credit counselor. Ask about qualifications, fees, alternatives, and whether creditors have accepted any proposed debt-management plan. Get the agreement in writing before paying. Debt settlement can involve missed payments, additional fees, lawsuits, credit damage, and possible tax consequences. If repayment is not realistic, a bankruptcy attorney or legal-aid service can explain options; signing an unaffordable plan does not make the underlying shortfall disappear.

- **Cost and effort:** Initial counseling may be free or low-cost; estimate an hour. Ongoing plan fees and legal fees vary, so obtain a written quote.
- **Payoff:** Helps distinguish an affordable repayment arrangement from a sales pitch and exposes consequences before you commit.
- **Priority:** When relevant.
- **Basis:** Guidance; Practice. [CFPB: Choosing credit counseling](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-credit-counseling-en-1451/), [CFPB: Debt-relief risks](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-debt-relief-program-and-how-do-i-know-if-i-should-use-one-en-1457/).

## 08.5 Interrupt a scam and act quickly after a loss

Stop the conversation when someone pressures you to send money or reveal account credentials. Verify the organization through a number you independently find. If you already paid, contact the bank, card issuer, or payment service immediately and ask whether the transaction can be stopped or reversed. Save messages and receipts, secure exposed accounts, and follow the FTC's recovery steps. Ignore unsolicited offers to recover the money for an upfront fee; recovery is never guaranteed.

- **Cost and effort:** Reporting is free; start immediately. Estimate 30 minutes for first calls, with more time if accounts or identity documents need repair.
- **Payoff:** Limits continued exposure and gives available recovery channels a chance to work before money moves further.
- **Priority:** When relevant.
- **Basis:** Guidance; Practice. [FTC: What to do if you were scammed](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-do-if-you-were-scammed), [FTC: Refund and recovery scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/refund-and-recovery-scams).
