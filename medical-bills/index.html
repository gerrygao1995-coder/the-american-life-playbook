A little structure helps

One problem.
A clear next move.

A large balance can mean several different things: a billing mistake, an insurance decision, an unaffordable but accurate charge, or a self-pay bill above an estimate. Start with the closest match below. You may need two paths at once. This guide helps you organize the next conversation; it does not promise a discount or decide what you legally owe.

How to use this kit +
  • Put the bill, insurance notices, receipts, and any estimate in one folder. Record the date each notice arrived.
  • Write down every payment, appeal, dispute, and court deadline separately. A phone call or request for a hold does not automatically extend any of them.
  • Use verified provider and plan contact details. Keep copies, submission confirmations, names, and reference numbers.

Path 01

The bill and EOB do not match

Start here if

You used insurance, but the provider's bill differs from the plan's explanation of benefits, omits payments, or lists unfamiliar services.

Your next moves

  1. 1

    Match the same claim

    An explanation of benefits (EOB) is not a bill. Match the patient, provider, service date, and claim number. Use the latest EOB; a corrected claim can generate another version. Its patient-responsibility amount may not subtract copays or deposits you already paid.

    Sources: CMS: Read an explanation of benefits

  2. 2

    Ask for the underlying detail

    Request an itemized bill and a payment history. Check services, quantities, insurance payments, contractual adjustments, and your receipts. Flag suspected duplicate charges or care you did not receive. Ask the office to explain the coding; unfamiliar wording alone does not establish an error.

    Sources: CMS: Check your medical bill for errors

  3. 3

    Give each office a specific task

    Ask billing to explain or correct the difference. Ask the insurer whether the claim was processed and whether another submission is needed. If coverage was denied, also use the denial path. Agree on a follow-up date and request written confirmation of any temporary billing hold.

    Sources: CMS: Check your medical bill for errors · HealthCare.gov: Internal appeals

  4. 4

    Close the loop

    Save the revised bill and EOB, and check that the correction or payment posted to the right account. If the balance remains unclear, ask a patient advocate or state Consumer Assistance Program for help. Keep the payment deadline visible while the offices investigate.

    Sources: CMS: Check your medical bill for errors

Words you can use

Call or secure-message script

For account [number], service date [date], my bill shows [amount] and my EOB shows [amount]. I also paid [amount]. Please explain the difference and send the itemized bill and payment history. Will you place a temporary hold while you review it? Please confirm what the hold covers and when it ends.

Know the limits

  • A mismatch is a reason to investigate, not proof that the whole bill disappears.
  • Requesting a hold does not itself stop collections, late fees, or an appeal deadline. Get the agreement in writing and continue tracking notices.

Path 02

Insurance denied the claim

Start here if

An EOB or denial letter says a service was excluded, not medically necessary, unauthorized, or otherwise unpaid.

Your next moves

  1. 1

    Identify the reason and the deadline

    Save the denial notice and request the cited plan provision. Check whether the problem is missing information, claim processing, or a coverage decision. For plans covered by the ACA appeals rules described by HealthCare.gov, an internal appeal is generally due within 180 days after receiving the denial.

    Sources: HealthCare.gov: Internal appeals

  2. 2

    Submit evidence that answers the reason

    Ask your clinician for relevant records or a letter addressing the stated denial. Include your identifiers, claim number, requested correction, and supporting documents. Follow the plan's submission instructions and keep proof. Confirm whether a provider's submission also preserves your own appeal rights.

    Sources: HealthCare.gov: Internal appeals

  3. 3

    Escalate when appropriate

    If an internal appeal fails, follow the final notice for external review. HealthCare.gov describes a four-month filing window for the processes it covers. Eligibility depends on the denial and plan; medical-judgment denials are a common example. In an urgent health situation, ask immediately about expedited review and whether internal and external review can proceed at the same time. Do not assume you must wait for the ordinary internal process to finish.

    Sources: HealthCare.gov: External review · HealthCare.gov: Internal appeals

  4. 4

    Use the right coverage system

    Medicare has separate appeal paths for Original Medicare, health plans, and drug plans; SHIP offers free counseling. For Medicaid, use your state agency and plan's notices to identify the applicable review process. Do not substitute this guide's commercial-plan deadlines for either program's instructions.

    Sources: Medicare: Filing an appeal · Medicaid: Where to get help

Words you can use

Appeal-preparation script

I want to appeal denial [claim number]. Please identify the exact reason, plan provision, filing deadline, and submission method. What records would address the reason? Does the provider's pending review count as my appeal, or must I file separately? Please send the instructions in writing.

Know the limits

  • A billing inquiry, corrected claim, complaint, and formal appeal are different processes; one may not replace another.
  • External review is not available for every dispute. Plan type, state procedures, and urgent-care rules matter. Keep pursuing billing assistance while preserving appeal rights.

Path 03

The bill is accurate, but you cannot afford it

Start here if

After checking coverage and charges, paying the remaining balance would strain basic living expenses.

Your next moves

  1. 1

    Request the actual assistance policy

    Ask for financial assistance or charity care, including the application and plain-language summary. Tax-exempt nonprofit hospitals must maintain a financial assistance policy (FAP). Eligibility and discounts depend on the policy. Ask which separately billing clinicians are covered; the hospital's program does not automatically cover every doctor.

    Sources: IRS: Financial assistance policies, Section 501(r)(4)

  2. 2

    Apply with a complete record

    For covered tax-exempt hospital care, you can apply as soon as care is provided. The protected application period generally runs through at least 240 days after the hospital sends the first post-discharge bill; some notices extend it. Apply promptly. Follow the document checklist, ask about alternatives for unavailable documents, and keep a receipt. Ask for a written decision and reconsideration process. Having insurance does not automatically rule out assistance.

    Sources: IRS: Financial assistance policies, Section 501(r)(4) · CFPB: If you cannot pay a medical bill · 26 CFR 1.501(r)-1: Financial assistance application period

  3. 3

    Ask which collection protections apply

    A timely, complete FAP application requires suspension of extraordinary collection actions, such as a lawsuit over the bill, while eligibility is determined. A timely incomplete application also triggers protection: the hospital must identify missing information and suspend those actions while giving you a reasonable opportunity to complete it. Respond promptly to the missing-document notice. These protections are not a blanket pause on every billing contact.

    Sources: IRS: Billing and collections, Section 501(r)(6) · 26 CFR 1.501(r)-6: Billing and collection protections

  4. 4

    Compare repayment only after assistance

    For a remaining balance, request an affordable, interest-free plan and written terms. Check fees, missed-payment consequences, and who owns the debt. A medical credit card can carry deferred interest and make assistance harder to obtain. Ask about assistance even if you already paid; payment alone does not end the inquiry.

    Sources: CFPB: If you cannot pay a medical bill · CFPB: Medical credit cards and payment plans

Words you can use

Financial-assistance script

I cannot afford this balance. Please send your financial assistance policy, application, and list of covered providers. What documents do you need, and where can I get application help? Please confirm receipt, whether my application is complete, and which billing or collection actions are paused during review.

Path 04

You are uninsured or paying without insurance

Start here if

You told the provider in advance that you were not using insurance, and the bill is higher than expected.

Your next moves

  1. 1

    Find or request the estimate

    For future scheduled care, request a written good faith estimate. Usually this is available when care is scheduled at least three business days ahead or on request. Ask each provider and facility separately; one estimate may not cover everyone. Emergency care does not come with this advance-estimate protection.

    Sources: CMS: Rights when not using insurance

  2. 2

    Check the threshold provider by provider

    Federal patient-provider dispute resolution (PPDR) may apply to eligible care received on or after January 1, 2022 when one provider or facility bills at least $400 above its good faith estimate. Compare that provider's totals; do not add unrelated providers' smaller differences together.

    Sources: CMS: Dispute a medical bill · 45 CFR 149.620: Patient-provider dispute resolution

  3. 3

    Preserve the 120-day window

    The regulation requires initiation within 120 calendar days of receiving the initial bill containing the disputed charges. Save the bill and receipt date, and file early. Use the CMS dispute page, submit the estimate and bill, and follow its current $25 nonrefundable administrative-fee instructions. Negotiating with billing does not extend the filing window.

    Sources: 45 CFR 149.620: Patient-provider dispute resolution · CMS: Dispute a medical bill

  4. 4

    Track the formal process

    During PPDR, the provider must pause applicable collection efforts, cannot send the disputed bill to collections, and cannot retaliate. Late-fee protections also apply. Save the official case notices and respond to requests for missing information. If you lack a written estimate, ask CMS about a complaint and use the affordability path.

    Sources: CMS: Payment resolution with patients · CMS: Rights when not using insurance

Estimate-discrepancy script

I told your office I was paying without insurance. Your written estimate was [amount], but this bill is [amount]. Please explain the additional charges and review the bill. I am also checking the federal dispute deadline. Please send any proposed adjustment and temporary hold terms in writing.

Know the limits

  • A qualifying difference does not guarantee a reduction: medically necessary, genuinely unforeseen care can affect the outcome.
  • Estimate timing and eligibility still matter. Ask the CMS No Surprises Help Desk, 1-800-985-3059, if uncertain; do not wait until the deadline.
  • Medicare and Medicaid have separate protections and procedures. This self-pay path is not a way to bypass those programs' rules.
  • The CMS consumer checklist refers to the bill date. Keep proof of receipt and contact CMS promptly if the dates create an eligibility question.

Sources: CMS: Rights when not using insurance · CMS: Dispute a medical bill · 45 CFR 149.620: Patient-provider dispute resolution · CMS: Payment resolution with patients · CMS: Provider requirements and resources

Before moving on

Keep these boundaries in view.

“We put in a note, so you can ignore the deadline.”

Request the hold's scope and end date in writing. Keep filing deadlines separate. An informal request is not the same as a formal appeal, PPDR case, or qualifying FAP application.

Sources: HealthCare.gov: Internal appeals · 45 CFR 149.620: Patient-provider dispute resolution · IRS: Billing and collections, Section 501(r)(6)

A collector demands money for a disputed balance

Verify the collector, obtain the validation notice, and use its deadline. For covered debt collectors, a timely written dispute within the 30-day validation period stops collection until verification. Keep delivery proof. Respond to court papers separately; an agency complaint is not a court response.

Sources: CFPB: Disputing debt with a collector

“Put it on this card now and sort it out later.”

Ask about assistance first. Check deferred interest and total financing costs; a promotional payment is not the full cost. Never promise an installment you cannot sustain.

Sources: CFPB: Medical credit cards and payment plans

An unexpected out-of-network charge

Some emergency, in-network-facility, and air-ambulance bills have federal protections. A high deductible alone is not a violation, and ground ambulances generally fall outside the federal No Surprises Act. Call CMS at 1-800-985-3059 for the appropriate route.

Sources: CMS: Medical bill rights · CFPB: If you cannot pay a medical bill · CMS: Dispute a medical bill

If you are having a medical emergency, call 911. Do not delay emergency care to finish billing paperwork.

Two fictional examples.

Illustrations of the process.

01Fictional worked example

The bill omitted a payment

Jordan receives a $340 bill. The matching EOB assigns Jordan $240, and a receipt shows a $40 payment for that visit. These invented numbers illustrate reconciliation, not a reported patient result.

What the person does

  • Jordan checks that all three documents cover the same provider and service date.
  • Jordan asks billing to reconcile the apparent difference and credit the $40 receipt, while confirming whether the EOB is final.
  • Jordan requests written confirmation and keeps the bill deadline on the calendar.
What this illustrates

If the $240 responsibility is final and the $40 applies to it, the arithmetic suggests $200 remains. Billing must verify those assumptions; the example does not establish the actual amount owed.

Sources: CMS: Read an explanation of benefits · CMS: Check your medical bill for errors

02Fictional worked example

One estimate, one larger bill

Sam, uninsured, tells a clinic in advance and receives a written $700 estimate well before scheduled care in 2026. The same clinic later bills $1,180. The $480 difference exceeds $400.

What the person does

  • Sam saves the estimate, initial bill, and date received, and checks CMS eligibility.
  • Sam asks for an explanation without letting negotiation consume the 120-day window.
  • If eligible, Sam follows the formal PPDR instructions and tracks official notices.
What this illustrates

This example shows a possible dispute route, not a promised $480 saving. Eligibility and the payment decision depend on the actual record and any reasonably unforeseen, medically necessary services.

Sources: 45 CFR 149.620: Patient-provider dispute resolution · CMS: Dispute a medical bill
